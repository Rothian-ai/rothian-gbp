Your people’s messaging apps are an attack surface. Treat them like one

NCSC, the FBI and the Dutch AIVD have exposed CHOSEN BRICK, Iranian spyware delivered through WhatsApp and Telegram conversations.

How it works: attackers pose as a known contact, build rapport over time and tailor the lure to the target. Then they persuade the person to install what looks like legitimate software, such as an AI video tool, antivirus or a password manager. The malware collects contacts, emails and messages, captures the screen, can reach the microphone and survives reboots. It talks to its operators through Telegram's API and commercial cloud storage, so it blends into normal traffic.

What to do about it:
1. Allow only approved, signed software to run for standard users. That stops the fake installer.
2. Brief the people most likely to be targeted: executives, their assistants, finance approvers, IT administrators and publicly visible staff. Give them a second channel to verify a contact.
3. Alert on unexpected connections to messaging-platform APIs and consumer cloud storage your business does not use.
4. Review run keys and scheduled tasks for entries that do not match approved software.
5. Set a clear messaging-app policy and make it easy to report a suspicious conversation. Banning the apps just pushes chats onto personal devices.

Ask your managed detection and response provider whether they have ingested the CHOSEN BRICK indicators.
